# Valley of Dragons IV: Land of Chivalry

> Fresh prose draft. Chapters 9–10 only. This draft is production material and is not accepted canon until reviewed.

## Chapter 9: Attack!

They saw the smoke before they saw Bellacourt.

At first Alaric thought it came from the city itself.

A dark line lay across the eastern horizon, too broad for a single fire and too low for a storm cloud.

Raymond rose in his saddle.

“That is near the river road.”

Gaston shaded his eyes.

“No,” he said. “Closer.”

The riders pushed their horses harder.

By midday they began to meet people fleeing west.

Farm carts. Shepherds. Merchants with half-loaded wagons. A priest riding beside three children on a mule.

Alaric stopped the priest.

“What happened?”

“Soldiers.”

“Whose?”

The priest shook his head.

“Not the king’s. They came before dawn. Took the east bridge, burned the watch house, cut the road.”

Raymond looked at Alaric.

“Lothar.”

The name no longer sounded distant.

A second group of refugees arrived from the north road.

Their story was worse.

Two warning beacons had never been lit.

One local captain had ordered his men to remain in barracks because a sealed royal message claimed the movements were only tournament traffic.

Another patrol had been sent twenty miles in the wrong direction.

None of it was enough by itself to open a kingdom.

Together, it created hours.

And hours were what an army needed.

Raymond swore.

“Bernier.”

Gaston nodded grimly.

“He did not improvise this after the feast.”

“No,” Alaric said. “He was already moving pieces.”

The riders reached the first royal outpost before sunset.

It had been abandoned in haste.

A broken signal horn lay beside the gate.

Inside, they found two wounded soldiers and a lieutenant trying to organize a dozen men.

“How close?” Raymond asked.

The lieutenant stared at him in disbelief.

“My lord? We were told you had been taken.”

“I was.”

The man seemed unsure what to do with that answer.

Raymond pointed east.

“How close is Lothar?”

“His vanguard crossed the river this morning. Main force behind. Infantry, cavalry, wagons. More men than we have here by twenty times.”

“Bellacourt warned?”

“We sent a rider.”

“Did he arrive?”

The lieutenant hesitated.

“We do not know.”

Alaric looked at the roads.

Bellacourt lay behind them to the west.

The enemy was moving from the east and northeast.

If they returned immediately, they might enter the city before the roads closed.

If they remained to help the outpost, they risked being cut off outside.

Raymond made the choice first.

“We go home.”

Alaric nodded.

“Agreed.”

They rode through the night.

Bellacourt appeared at dawn.

Its walls were already crowded.

Banners snapped above the towers. Carts jammed the road to the gates. Soldiers shouted at peasants and merchants trying to enter at once.

Beyond the eastern fields, cavalry moved like dark insects along the ridges.

Lothar’s scouts.

The city gates remained open just long enough.

Raymond crossed first.

Alaric followed with the Dragon Riders.

The gate captain recognized them and shouted for the portcullis to remain raised.

A minute later, the last wagon squeezed through.

Then the chains groaned.

The gate came down.

Outside, a horn answered from the hills.

Bellacourt was not yet surrounded.

But it soon would be.

Charles was waiting in Côte de Lys.

He had not slept.

Maps covered the same tables where, only days earlier, Anselm had organized the response to the sickness.

The similarities were impossible to miss.

Except now the marks on the maps were soldiers.

Raymond entered first.

Charles crossed the room and embraced him.

For a moment the king said nothing.

Then he stepped back.

“You were told to remain inside the citadel.”

Raymond looked at Alaric.

“Can we postpone that conversation until after the invasion?”

“No.”

Charles stared at him.

Then he exhaled.

“Yes.”

Alaric almost smiled.

Almost.

“What do we know?” he asked.

Charles pointed to the map.

“Too much and not enough.”

Lothar’s army had crossed into the kingdom along three roads.

The fastest warning route had been sabotaged.

Two small forts had received forged orders and yielded their bridges without a fight.

Several lesser nobles loyal to Bernier had delayed mobilizing.

Others had obeyed Charles immediately but could not concentrate their men in time.

The tournament itself had made things worse.

Hundreds of knights and nobles were in Bellacourt.

That gave Charles more trained fighters inside the capital.

It also meant their household troops were scattered across distant estates.

“A city full of knights,” Charles said bitterly, “and half their men are three days away.”

“Then we hold three days,” Alaric said.

Charles looked at him.

“Can we?”

Alaric studied the walls.

He had defended Castle Tarn against monsters.

He had fought in mountain passes, forests and ruins.

A human army was different.

Humans brought engineers.

Schedules.

Supply wagons.

Siege tools.

They also grew tired, argued and made mistakes.

“Bellacourt is strong,” Alaric said. “And Lothar moved quickly. Quick armies outrun things.”

“Such as?”

“Food. Heavy equipment. Reinforcements.”

Charles nodded.

That was the first useful thing anyone had said all morning.

By afternoon, Lothar closed the eastern road.

By evening, cavalry appeared south of the city.

By midnight, the northern gate was under observation.

Bellacourt was besieged.

The first assault came the next morning.

It was not the great attack Alaric had expected.

Lothar tested the walls.

Archers moved forward behind large shields. Infantry pushed ladders toward a lesser tower. Men with axes tried to damage an outer gate while cavalry remained beyond bowshot.

Charles’s defenders drove them back.

The city cheered.

Alaric did not.

“He wanted to see how quickly we reacted,” he said.

Raymond stood beside him on the wall.

“And now he knows.”

“Yes.”

“Comforting.”

The second day brought siege engines.

Not many.

Enough.

Small stone-throwers began striking the eastern wall. Work crews filled ditches beneath covering fire. Lothar’s men cut trees from nearby orchards and farms.

Bellacourt answered with bolts, stones, burning pitch and cavalry sorties through the western gate while the road remained partly open.

By the third day, even that road was threatened.

The siege tightened.

The city changed quickly.

Markets became ration points.

Guild halls became workshops for arrows, shields and repairs.

Monasteries became hospitals.

The same merchants Alaric had watched arguing over cloth prices now counted sacks of grain for the walls.

The same monks who had carried water west now carried wounded men through the streets.

The same systems he had spent days examining were suddenly under pressure.

Some bent.

Some failed.

Many worked.

At Notre-Dame du Lys, Archbishop Anselm ordered the cathedral crypts opened for civilians from damaged neighborhoods.

When a noble protested that his family should receive priority quarters, Anselm replied that stone did not recognize heraldry when it fell from the sky.

Alaric heard about it later and laughed for the first time in days.

On the fourth evening, Lothar sent a messenger.

The man rode beneath a white flag and asked to speak to Charles from beyond the east gate.

Charles went to the wall with Raymond, Alaric and Anselm.

Across the field stood a group of armored riders.

At their center was a broad-shouldered man in a dark cloak.

Warlord Lothar.

Beside him stood Count Bernier.

Bernier looked smaller than Alaric remembered.

Lothar raised his voice.

“Charles of Bretonnia!”

Charles stepped forward.

“I am here.”

“You have one day to open the gates.”

“And then?”

“Your soldiers lay down their arms. Your nobles swear new oaths. You retain your estates.”

Raymond muttered, “Generous.”

Lothar continued.

“Your cousin will be confirmed as heir under guarantees agreed with your lords.”

Raymond leaned over the wall.

“I am standing here!”

There was a brief silence across the battlefield.

Several defenders laughed.

Even Charles smiled.

Lothar did not.

Bernier looked away.

The failed kidnapping had reached him.

Alaric watched his face.

For the first time, he understood something.

Bernier had expected to present Raymond as a captive piece on a political board.

Instead Raymond was shouting insults from a wall.

One entire part of the plan was gone.

But the army remained.

Charles called down.

“You crossed my borders under false orders, attacked my people and surrounded my capital. There will be no oath.”

Lothar’s answer was calm.

“Then the city pays for your pride.”

Bernier turned toward him sharply.

It was a small movement.

Alaric noticed.

The delegation rode away.

Anselm remained at the wall.

“He said the city pays.”

“Yes,” Charles said.

“He did not say the crown pays.”

“No.”

Bernier had heard it too.

That night the bombardment intensified.

A stone struck a house near the eastern gate and killed three people.

Another broke part of a tower stair.

Fires burned in two districts before guild bucket lines contained them.

Bellacourt could hold.

But not forever.

Charles’s outside armies were gathering.

Too slowly.

The nearest force strong enough to challenge Lothar was still days away.

And Lothar knew it.

Alaric stood over the map with Charles.

“There is one force that could arrive faster.”

Charles knew before he said it.

“The dragons.”

“The Valley.”

“Can they reach us?”

“If someone reaches them.”

They both looked toward the western road.

By then, Lothar’s scouts watched it from the hills.

The city was not completely sealed.

But leaving was nearly as dangerous as entering.

“We need a courier,” Charles said.

Raymond entered the room.

“I will go.”

“No,” Charles and Alaric said together.

Raymond looked offended.

Alaric pointed at him.

“We just recovered you.”

“This is becoming tiresome.”

“You are the heir.”

“I am also a rider.”

“An average one.”

Raymond stared.

“Cruel.”

Charles ignored them.

A young royal courier named Martin volunteered.

He knew the western farms, side roads and old hunting tracks better than any knight present.

The plan was simple.

At midnight, Bellacourt would launch a short cavalry sortie from the western gate.

Lothar’s scouts would move to intercept.

Martin would leave separately through a postern farther north, ride through vineyard paths and cross the river above the main road.

Then he would ride for the Valley.

Alaric gave him a small token of the Dragon Rider Order.

“Show this at Castle Tarn. Ask for Gawain.”

Martin turned it over in his hand.

“And if they do not believe me?”

“Tell them Alaric says Vaelor still owes him three sheep.”

Martin frowned.

“Does he?”

“No.”

“Then why—”

“He will know I sent you.”

The courier smiled nervously.

At midnight the gates opened.

Hooves thundered into darkness.

Arrows answered from the hills.

For ten minutes the western fields became chaos.

Then the riders returned.

Three horses without riders.

Two wounded men.

No Martin.

No one knew whether that meant success or death.

Bellacourt could only wait.

## Chapter 10: Breaking the Siege

The fifth day passed.

Then the sixth.

No dragons came.

Lothar’s army built heavier engines.

The eastern wall cracked near one tower.

Defenders filled the gap with timber, carts, stones and anything else that could hold weight.

Food remained sufficient.

Arrows did not.

Sleep became something people borrowed in short pieces.

Alaric spent more time on the walls than in Côte de Lys.

Without Vaelor, he felt incomplete.

He hated admitting that.

For years, dragon and rider had fought as one unit.

Now every time Alaric looked over a wall, he saw the battlefield the way a dragon would see it.

Open fields.

Siege engines.

Supply wagons.

Dense formations.

All waiting for fire from above.

And above them was only sky.

On the seventh morning, Lothar attacked in force.

Drums began before sunrise.

Thousands of men moved toward the walls beneath banners Alaric did not recognize.

Archers darkened the air.

Ladders rose.

Siege engines struck the eastern defenses again and again.

At the damaged tower, part of the outer wall collapsed.

The breach was not wide.

It was wide enough.

Lothar’s infantry rushed it.

Alaric met them there.

For an hour the battle became stone, mud, smoke and steel.

There was no room for elegant swordsmanship.

Men pushed shields against shields.

Spears stabbed through gaps.

Defenders hauled wounded men backward while fresh soldiers took their places.

Alaric fought beside Bretonnian knights who days earlier had laughed at the Dragon Riders’ poor jousting.

Now no one cared how anyone sat a horse.

Master Bertrand appeared beside him with blood on one sleeve.

“How is the riding?” he shouted.

Alaric blocked a spear.

“Improving!”

“Good!”

Then Bertrand shoved a man off the rubble with his shield.

The defenders held the breach.

Barely.

By afternoon, Lothar withdrew.

The field outside Bellacourt was covered with broken ladders, abandoned shields and wounded men from both sides.

The city bells rang.

Not victory.

Warning.

Because everyone knew he would attack again.

In Lothar’s camp, Bernier finally argued openly with him.

Alaric did not hear the conversation.

Later, prisoners described it.

Bernier demanded that the city be spared once the walls fell.

Lothar asked why.

“Because it is the capital,” Bernier said.

“Exactly,” Lothar answered.

A city that resisted had to learn what resistance cost.

Bernier had imagined a change of rulers, alliances and influence.

Lothar saw wealth, submission and fear.

By then, Bernier understood the difference.

Too late.

On the eighth day, the western horizon remained empty.

Charles joined Alaric on the wall.

“You think the courier failed.”

“I think nothing.”

“That is unlike you.”

“I am trying something new.”

Charles looked over the fields.

Lothar’s men were preparing another assault.

“Would Vaelor come?”

Alaric turned toward him.

“If he knows?”

“Yes.”

“Nothing could stop him.”

Charles nodded.

“That is almost reassuring.”

Below them, people moved through the streets carrying water and stones.

A group of monks helped reinforce a gate.

Guild apprentices carried bundles of arrows from workshops.

Noble retainers hauled carts beside fishermen and bakers.

The estates, guilds and institutions of Bretonnia had become less distinct under siege.

Not gone.

But rearranged by necessity.

Anselm approached the wall.

He carried no ceremonial robes now.

Only a dark cloak over simple armor someone had convinced him to wear.

“The hospital crypts are full,” he said. “We can open the eastern chapter house if needed.”

Charles nodded.

Anselm looked at Alaric.

“Still nothing?”

“No.”

The Archbishop looked toward the west.

Then toward the enemy camp.

“I find myself hoping for dragons.”

Alaric smiled faintly.

“Careful.”

“I said hoping. Not approving.”

“Of course.”

At noon the drums began again.

This assault was larger than the first.

Lothar had moved engines close to the walls overnight.

Under heavy covering fire, men advanced with shields, ladders and hooked poles.

At the eastern breach, defenders braced behind new timber barricades.

At the south gate, another force pushed a ram forward beneath a wet hide roof.

Alaric ran between positions until he no longer knew which direction the city lay.

The south gate shook.

Once.

Twice.

Again.

The hinges held.

A fourth strike splintered part of the inner beam.

Raymond stood above the gatehouse throwing stones down with everyone else.

“Still think I should stay inside the citadel?”

“Yes!” Alaric shouted.

“Wonderful!”

Another blow struck the gate.

Then a sound rolled across the city.

Deep.

Distant.

Not a bell.

Not thunder.

Alaric stopped moving.

Around him, men continued fighting.

No one else understood yet.

The sound came again.

A roar.

Alaric looked west.

At first he saw nothing.

Then a black shape crossed the sun.

Another followed.

Then five.

Then twelve.

More appeared behind them.

The sky filled with wings.

Someone on the western wall shouted.

The cry spread faster than fire.

“DRAGONS!”

Lothar’s soldiers heard it too.

The attack faltered.

Men looked upward.

For most of them, dragons belonged to stories.

For some, monsters.

For a few, gods.

None had expected them to arrive as a disciplined army.

At the front flew a sapphire-blue dragon.

Vaelor.

Alaric laughed aloud.

Vaelor folded his wings and descended.

Behind him came dragons of red, green, silver and colors Alaric could not see through the smoke.

Dragon Rider Knights filled the sky above Bellacourt.

Not all sixty.

Enough.

The first pass struck Lothar’s siege engines.

Dragons did not burn the infantry packed closest to the walls.

The riders had chosen their targets carefully.

Fire swept across empty engine crews, timber towers, supply wagons and ammunition stores after warning passes scattered the men around them.

A stone-thrower vanished beneath a burst of flame.

Another dragon shattered the roof over the south gate’s battering ram with a controlled blast.

The soldiers carrying it dropped the beam and ran.

Bellacourt erupted in cheers.

Then the gates opened.

Charles had been waiting for that moment.

Bretonnian cavalry poured out from the western and northern gates while Lothar’s formations struggled to face two directions at once.

The Dragon Riders attacked from above.

Charles’s knights struck from the city.

Outside forces that had been gathering beyond the siege lines finally advanced as well, seeing the signal and hearing the horns.

Lothar’s carefully organized army began to come apart.

Alaric ran toward the inner court.

Vaelor landed hard enough to scatter dust across the stones.

Alaric stopped in front of him.

For one second neither moved.

Then Vaelor lowered his head.

Alaric pressed his forehead against the dragon’s scales.

“You took your time.”

Vaelor growled.

Gawain slid from the saddle.

“The message arrived three days ago.”

“Three?”

“The courier nearly killed two horses reaching us.”

“Martin?”

“Alive.”

Alaric exhaled.

Gawain looked toward the battle.

“Do you intend to stand here?”

“No.”

Alaric climbed into the saddle.

The world changed immediately.

The courtyard fell away.

Walls became lines.

Streets became patterns.

The enemy camp spread beneath him.

He could see Lothar’s reserves moving east.

He could see supply wagons trying to escape.

He could see Bernier’s banners near the command tents.

“Vaelor,” Alaric said.

The dragon banked.

They struck the camp.

Not the tents themselves.

The road beyond them.

Vaelor’s fire cut across a line of wagons and forced Lothar’s retreating cavalry away from the northern route.

Other dragons descended in coordinated passes.

The siege became a rout.

By sunset, Lothar’s army was retreating east.

Not destroyed.

Broken enough.

Bernier did not escape with the first wave.

Royal cavalry found him near an abandoned command wagon with a handful of retainers.

He surrendered.

When Charles saw him later, bound but unharmed, Bernier looked less like the elegant court conspirator from the feast and more like a tired old man.

“You brought him here,” Charles said.

Bernier did not answer.

“You opened roads to him. Delayed warnings. Lied to men who trusted seals bearing my name.”

“I did.”

“And then discovered he did not intend to make you his partner.”

Bernier looked toward the smoke outside the walls.

“No.”

Charles’s expression hardened.

“That discovery does not absolve you.”

“I know.”

His final judgment would come later.

For now there were wounded to count, fires to extinguish and walls to repair.

As evening fell, the dragons landed outside Bellacourt.

The reaction was not what Alaric expected.

People came out slowly at first.

Soldiers.

Then children.

Then families who had spent days in crypts and cellars.

Some were frightened.

Some crossed themselves.

Some simply stared.

Anselm walked through the eastern gate.

He stopped in front of Vaelor.

Vaelor watched him.

The Archbishop watched Vaelor.

Alaric waited.

Anselm had spent weeks asking whether dragons belonged inside the moral order he understood.

Now the answer stood before him with soot on its scales after helping save thousands of people who had once feared it.

Anselm removed his gloves.

Then, slowly, he placed one hand against Vaelor’s snout.

Vaelor did not move.

The crowd fell quiet.

Anselm turned toward the people gathered behind him.

“We judged what we did not understand.”

No one spoke.

“We feared power because we did not know its purpose.”

He looked back at Vaelor.

“Today, that power was used in defense of this city, its people and its churches.”

Alaric said nothing.

This was not the moment to win an argument.

Anselm continued.

“The Church will examine what this means. We will not pretend that every question is answered in a single day.”

That sounded like him.

Then his voice changed.

“But neither will we deny what our own eyes have seen.”

He bowed his head toward Vaelor.

“Welcome to Bellacourt.”

For several seconds, silence remained.

Then someone began to cheer.

Others joined.

The sound spread across the gate, the walls and the fields where dragons rested among broken siege engines.

Alaric looked toward Côte de Lys.

The city still smoked.

Its walls were damaged.

Its people were exhausted.

But Bellacourt stood.

And above it, for the first time, the banners of Bretonnia and the Dragon Rider Order flew together.

## Chapter 5: The Capital Markets

The morning after the first days of jousting practice, Alaric discovered that Bellacourt had another kind of tournament.

It took place in the markets.

Gaston had promised to show the Dragon Riders how the capital actually lived when no one was standing in a royal hall or charging down a tournament lane. He met them shortly after sunrise beneath one of the inner gates of Côte de Lys.

“No horses?” Alaric asked.

“Not unless you want to spend the entire morning trying not to trample apprentices.”

They went on foot.

Bellacourt was already awake.

Carts entered through the gates in a steady line. Farmers brought sacks of grain, baskets of onions, wheels of cheese, smoked fish, live chickens and barrels of cider. Porters shouted for space. Shopkeepers opened shutters. Bells announced the beginning of work in some quarters while church bells called worshippers elsewhere.

Alaric had seen large markets before. Riventhorn could be crowded on trading days, and dwarven caravans had made the Valley richer and busier in recent years.

But Bellacourt was different because almost everything seemed organized into groups.

They passed a street of metalworkers, then one of leatherworkers, then another where almost every doorway belonged to cloth merchants. Painted signs hung over the doors: shears, hammers, boots, loaves, keys, horseshoes.

“Guilds,” Gaston explained.

Alaric stopped beside a shop where three apprentices were carrying finished locks into the morning light.

“Like a craftsmen’s association?”

“More than that. A guild decides who may practice a trade in the city, who may become a master, what standards must be kept, how apprentices are trained, and often what prices and measures are considered fair.”

Alaric raised an eyebrow.

“They decide who may work?”

“In that trade, yes.”

“That sounds useful and dangerous at the same time.”

Gaston smiled. “You are beginning to understand Bretonnia.”

They entered a covered hall where cloth merchants displayed bolts of dyed wool and linen beneath a roof supported by thick wooden beams. Buyers inspected fabric, argued over quality and called for officials to check weights.

One merchant greeted Gaston by name and bowed deeply when he learned who Alaric was.

“A Dragon Rider Knight in my shop,” the man said. “My sons will never believe it.”

Alaric looked at the cloth stacked behind him.

“Did you make these?”

The merchant laughed.

“No, my lord. I buy from weavers, dyers and fullers. Some nearby, some far away.”

“So you move goods between people who make them.”

“And take the risk that no one will buy them when they arrive.”

That Alaric understood.

He had worked with caravans before he became a knight. He remembered wet roads, broken wheels, spoiled food, poor prices and merchants trying to predict what a town would need weeks before reaching it.

The merchant showed him a piece of fine blue cloth.

“This came from three days east of here. The wool came from somewhere else. The dye from farther still.”

Alaric ran the fabric between his fingers.

A finished object, he realized, could carry half a kingdom inside it.

Outside, Gaston pointed toward the scales in the center of the square.

“The Crown guarantees the official measures here.”

“And collects taxes.”

“Also that.”

They continued toward the food markets.

There the difference between city and countryside became impossible to miss.

Bellacourt consumed enormous quantities of food every day, yet little of it was grown within the walls. Grain came from estates and villages across the kingdom. Fish arrived from rivers and lakes. Cattle and sheep were driven from grazing country. Wine, salt and spices traveled even farther.

Alaric watched a baker purchase flour from a grain factor while a clerk made marks on a wax tablet.

“A city this large lives because thousands of people it never sees bring things to it,” he said.

Gaston nodded.

“And those villages rely on the city for tools, markets, protection, coin, courts and craftsmen they cannot support alone.”

Alaric thought of the Valley’s river cities and surrounding farms.

The principle was familiar.

The scale was not.

Near midday they reached a broad square dominated by a guildhall on one side and a church house on the other. A group of men in fine clothes stepped aside as the Dragon Riders passed.

They bowed.

A woman carrying two baskets did the same.

So did a young apprentice with flour on his sleeves.

Alaric slowed.

“Why does everyone keep doing that?”

Gaston looked puzzled.

“You are knights.”

“So?”

Gaston stared at him for a moment.

“In Bretonnia, that means something socially as well as militarily.”

Alaric looked back at the apprentice.

The boy was already hurrying away.

“In the Valley, people respect the Order because of what we do.”

“They respect knights here for what they are.”

That answer bothered him.

Gaston led them into a small eating house near the square. The owner insisted the Dragon Riders sit at the best table and refused payment until Alaric nearly argued with him.

“Another privilege?” Alaric asked once food arrived.

“Courtesy.”

“That is not what I asked.”

Gaston sighed.

“Both.”

He explained that Bretonnian society divided duties as much as honors. Nobles and knights were expected to fight, judge disputes, protect roads, administer estates and support the Church. Clergy prayed, taught, recorded, advised and cared for many of the poor and sick. Townspeople traded and practiced crafts. Farmers worked the land that fed everyone.

“In theory,” Gaston said, “each estate serves the others.”

“In theory,” Alaric repeated.

“Yes.”

“And in practice?”

Gaston tore a piece of bread.

“In practice, people are people.”

That afternoon they saw both sides.

At a guild charity house, widows of craftsmen received bread and small payments from a fund maintained by members of the trade. At a church hospital, brothers tended to travelers and poor citizens who could not afford a physician.

Alaric was impressed.

Then, two streets later, he watched a noble servant push a farmer’s cart aside because his lord’s carriage wished to pass.

The farmer protested.

The servant pointed to the coat of arms on the carriage.

The protest ended.

Alaric’s expression must have shown what he thought, because Gaston said quietly, “Do not mistake the ideal for the whole reality.”

“I’m trying not to.”

They climbed a hill toward a square where several guildhalls faced one another. Their upper floors were richly carved and painted. These were not poor craftsmen’s clubs. Some guilds controlled large amounts of wealth, hired guards, financed ships or caravans, and lent money to nobles.

“So a merchant may be richer than a knight?” Alaric asked.

“Much richer.”

“But the merchant bows to him.”

“Often.”

“Bretonnia is strange.”

“Every land is strange to someone.”

Alaric could not argue with that.

As evening approached, they passed Notre-Dame du Lys.

A line of people stood beside one of its side buildings. Monks distributed soup and bread. A clerk recorded donations on a long sheet of parchment. Nearby, two scholars in church robes argued over a book while a messenger waited with letters for another diocese.

Alaric remembered what Archbishop Anselm had said.

The Church was not simply a collection of priests who disliked dragons.

It connected places.

It kept records. It taught. It fed people. It preserved laws and stories. Its bishops could speak across borders that kings could not easily cross.

That made its caution understandable.

It also made its mistakes powerful.

If a village elder distrusted dragons, one village distrusted dragons.

If the Church declared them dangerous, half the Bretonnian Lands might believe it.

Alaric looked up at the cathedral tower.

For the first time, he understood why Charles had not simply ordered Anselm to stop objecting.

A king ruled a kingdom.

The Church reached beyond kingdoms.

On their return to Côte de Lys, the streets were turning gold in the evening light. Apprentices closed shutters. Farmers began the journey back beyond the walls. Bakers prepared for the next morning. Noble households sent servants to purchase supplies for another round of feasts and ceremonies.

Bellacourt no longer looked to Alaric like a great collection of stone buildings.

It looked like a machine made of people.

Farmers fed craftsmen. Craftsmen equipped knights. Merchants connected regions. Guilds controlled trades. Nobles controlled land. The Crown tried to hold them together. The Church crossed almost every boundary between them.

Each part had a purpose.

Each part also had power.

And every kind of power, Alaric had learned, could be used well or badly.

The Grand Tourney had brought him to Bellacourt to learn how Bretonnia fought.

The markets were teaching him how Bretonnia worked.

# Valley of Dragons IV: Land of Chivalry

## Chapter 3: The Vigil and the Knighting

Without the dragons, Bellacourt felt different.

Alaric noticed it most in the mornings.

For years, the beginning of almost every journey had meant the sound of wings, the weight of a saddle, and the familiar presence of Vaelor beside him. Now he woke beneath a painted ceiling in Côte de Lys, heard bells from Notre-Dame du Lys, and remembered that Vaelor was already far to the east.

The Bretonnians, however, seemed determined to keep him too busy to dwell on it.

The week before the Grand Tourney was filled with ceremonies. Knights arrived from distant duchies and neighboring kingdoms. Heralds registered coats of arms. Noble households filled the courtyards of Bellacourt with horses, servants, banners and wagons. Everywhere Alaric looked, some rule of chivalry seemed to be in progress.

At first he found much of it unnecessary.

In the Valley, a knight proved himself by what he could do and by whether others could trust him. The Dragon Rider Order trained its members hard, judged them by conduct, and expected them to serve. Ceremony existed, but it came after substance.

Bretonnia seemed to begin with ceremony and build outward from it.

Gaston tried to explain.

“Here, everyone needs to know who everyone else is,” he said as they crossed one of Bellacourt’s great courtyards. “A knight may serve a count, who owes fealty to a duke, who has an agreement with a bishop, who is related by marriage to a king who dislikes Charles. A shield can tell you more quickly than a conversation.”

“That sounds less like chivalry and more like bookkeeping.”

Gaston laughed.

“Sometimes it is both.”

Alaric soon met pages barely old enough to carry the helmets entrusted to them. They learned court manners, scripture, music, riding, heraldry and obedience. Older boys served as squires, caring for horses and armor while training with sword and lance.

One afternoon, Alaric stopped to watch a squire polish a knight’s breastplate until the steel shone like a mirror.

“How long have you served him?” Alaric asked.

“Five years, my lord.”

“And after that?”

“If God wills it and Sir Étienne judges me worthy, I may be knighted.”

Alaric frowned.

“What if he doesn’t?”

The boy looked genuinely confused by the question.

“Then I am not ready.”

Alaric thought of his own road to knighthood: his years as a craftsman, his training, the Order’s wars, his bond with Vaelor, and the moment when Gawain had finally raised him into the Order.

Different road, he thought. Perhaps not an easier one.

That evening King Charles invited the Dragon Riders to witness a knighting vigil at Notre-Dame du Lys.

The cathedral was even larger inside than it appeared from Bellacourt’s streets. Rows of pillars rose into shadow. Candles burned before painted saints and ancient kings. The Sacred Lily appeared everywhere: carved into stone, embroidered into banners and worked into colored glass.

The young man to be knighted entered without armor.

His sword was placed before the altar.

For most of the night he remained alone in prayer.

Alaric expected boredom. Instead he found himself impressed.

There were no cheering crowds, no tournament trumpets, no nobles displaying jewels. The vigil was deliberately quiet. The candidate was meant to consider what the sword was for, whom he would serve, and what kind of man he intended to become once people were required to call him knight.

Archbishop Anselm presided from a distance.

When he noticed Alaric watching, he approached.

“You seem surprised.”

“I thought Bretonnian knighthood was mostly banners and expensive horses.”

Anselm gave him a dry look.

“And I thought Dragon Riders were men who sat on monsters and burned their enemies from the sky.”

Alaric looked at him.

The Archbishop almost smiled.

“Perhaps,” Anselm said, “we are both learning.”

The next morning the cathedral filled.

The squire from the vigil knelt before his lord. A priest blessed the sword. Oaths were spoken: to defend the weak, uphold justice, remain faithful, honor rightful authority and keep one’s word.

Some of those promises sounded very familiar to Alaric.

Then came the accolade, and the young man rose as a knight.

Outside the cathedral, Bellacourt erupted into celebration.

Alaric walked beside Charles through the square.

“You approve?” the king asked.

“Of the oath, yes.”

“And the rest?”

Alaric glanced at the noble families surrounding the newly made knight.

“I’m still deciding.”

Charles nodded, apparently pleased by the answer.

Alaric had begun to understand the appeal of Bretonnian chivalry. It gave people a language for courage, duty and service. A child could watch a knight take his oath and understand that strength was supposed to mean responsibility.

But he could also see the danger.

Some boys had opportunities because their fathers possessed land and names. Some squires had been raised inside castles from childhood, while other children in Bellacourt would never come close to a sword except to repair one or carry it for someone richer.

The ideals were admirable.

Access to them was not equal.

That distinction stayed with Alaric as the Grand Tourney drew near.

He had expected to come to Bretonnia and show its knights what the Dragon Rider Order could do.

Instead, Bretonnia was already forcing him to reconsider what a knight was.

## Chapter 4: Their First Jousting

The Dragon Riders discovered their problem on the first morning of practice.

They did not know how to joust.

This surprised everyone, including them.

The tournament grounds outside Bellacourt had been transformed into a city of timber stands, colorful pavilions and long fenced lanes. Banners snapped above the field. Armorers worked beneath awnings. Stable boys hurried between rows of horses. Trumpeters practiced signals until even the horses seemed tired of hearing them.

Alaric stood beside a rack of tournament lances and lifted one.

It was longer than he expected.

“Too heavy?” asked a voice behind him.

Alaric turned.

The speaker was a broad-shouldered Bretonnian knight with a grey beard and the patient expression of a man accustomed to watching younger warriors make mistakes.

“Not heavy,” Alaric said.

“Good. Then you will have no excuse when you drop it.”

This was Master Bertrand, appointed by Charles to prepare the visiting Dragon Riders for the tourney.

Alaric liked him immediately.

The first exercise seemed simple. Mount the horse, lower the lance and strike a painted shield fixed to a post.

Alaric missed.

The lance passed neatly to the left.

Bertrand said nothing.

Alaric circled back and tried again.

This time he hit the edge and nearly twisted himself out of the saddle.

Bertrand nodded.

“Excellent.”

Alaric stared at him.

“I almost fell off.”

“Yes. Now you have begun to learn.”

The Dragon Riders were experienced warriors, but most of their mounted instincts came from dragons.

That turned out to be a disadvantage.

A dragon adjusted in the air beneath its rider. It banked, climbed and understood commands with an intelligence no horse possessed. A rider could shift his whole body during a turn and trust his dragon to answer.

A tournament horse required different balance. Different timing. Different restraint.

The lance made everything worse.

“You are all trying to ride as if the animal can read your mind,” Bertrand told them after an especially poor round.

“Ours usually can,” Alaric said.

“That is deeply unfair.”

By the third day, they were improving.

Bertrand taught them to lock their legs, carry the lance without fighting its weight, keep their eyes on the target and trust the horse’s line. They practiced against rings, posts and padded quintains before being allowed to charge another rider.

The first time Alaric faced a real opponent, he felt absurdly nervous.

He had fought demons.

He had crossed blades with monsters beneath mountains.

Yet now a cheerful knight from some duchy he had never heard of was galloping toward him with a wooden lance, and Alaric’s main concern was remembering not to lean as if Vaelor were about to bank left.

The impact exploded across his shield.

His own lance struck the other knight’s shoulder and shattered.

The crowd watching practice cheered.

Alaric remained in the saddle.

Bertrand raised both hands.

“A miracle.”

Alaric laughed despite himself.

The next lesson was heraldry.

This proved almost as complicated as jousting.

The Dragon Riders had their own symbols and colors, but the Bretonnian heralds insisted that tournament shields needed to be readable from a distance and distinct enough to identify each competitor immediately.

A royal herald laid sheets of painted designs across a table.

“Your personal arms,” he told Alaric, “should say who you are.”

“That sounds dangerous.”

“It is the entire purpose.”

Alaric looked through designs of beasts, stars, towers, swords, suns, rivers and trees.

In Bretonnia, heraldry was not decoration. It was identity made visible.

A knight’s shield could show family, marriage, allegiance, inheritance, victories and claims. Charles’s lilies were everywhere not simply because the king liked flowers, but because every repetition reminded people that his crown stood at the center of the realm.

The Dragon Riders’ arrival had created another problem.

How did one represent a knight whose defining companion had been ordered hundreds of leagues away?

Alaric considered a dragon.

Then he rejected it.

Not because he was ashamed of Vaelor, but because he did not want the tournament to become an argument painted onto his shield.

Instead, he chose symbols that connected the Valley and the Order without making a political challenge of them.

The other riders did the same in their own ways.

When the finished shields were carried onto the practice field, Bretonnian spectators gathered to study them as seriously as if they were reading official letters.

Alaric was beginning to understand why Gaston had called heraldry a language.

By the eve of the tourney, the riders could pass as competent jousters.

Competent, Alaric discovered, was very different from excellent.

From the stands he watched Bretonnian champions train.

They had practiced this since youth. They knew exactly how much of the horse’s speed could be turned into the lance. They struck shields at precise angles. They recovered from impacts that would have thrown Alaric sideways three days earlier.

Their skill impressed him.

There was something healthy in discovering that a famous warrior could travel far enough from home to become a beginner again.

Master Bertrand joined him at the rail.

“You expected to win easily.”

“I expected not to embarrass myself.”

“That is a more achievable ambition.”

Alaric smiled.

Across the field, the towers of Bellacourt rose behind the tournament grounds. Beyond them stood Côte de Lys and the spires of Notre-Dame du Lys.

For the moment, the city seemed peaceful.

The Church remained uncertain about the dragons. Charles continued receiving nobles and envoys from across the Bretonnian Lands. Every feast and ceremony carried meanings Alaric only partly understood.

But tomorrow, at least, the rules would be simple.

Two knights would ride toward one another.

One lance would meet one shield.

And Alaric, Dragon Rider Knight of the Valley, would try very hard not to fall off a horse.

## Chapter 5: The Capital Markets

The morning after the first rounds of the Grand Tourney, Alaric woke with an ache in his shoulder and a new respect for Bretonnian lances.

He had remained in the saddle.

That, Master Bertrand assured him, was already a respectable achievement.

“You struck your opponent cleanly,” Bertrand said over breakfast. “You merely struck him as if you were trying to move a mountain.”

“I usually fight things that need to be moved.”

“That explains a great deal.”

There were no jousts scheduled for Alaric until the following day, so Gaston offered to show him another part of Bellacourt.

“The tournament field shows you what Bretonnia thinks a knight should look like,” the herald said. “The markets show you what keeps him alive.”

Bellacourt’s markets seemed large enough to feed a kingdom.

Farmers entered through the gates before sunrise with carts of grain, onions, apples, cheeses, chickens and barrels of salted meat. Fishmongers shouted beside bakers. Cloth merchants displayed wool, linen and imported silks. Smiths sold tools beside craftsmen whose work Alaric could not even identify.

The streets were organized by trade. One lane smelled of leather. Another rang with hammers. A third was filled with dyers whose vats turned water red, yellow and deep blue.

Alaric stopped beneath a wooden sign painted with a boot.

“Why are all the shoemakers here?”

“Because this is the shoemakers’ quarter.”

“I can see that.”

“They belong to a guild.”

Gaston explained that the guild decided who could open a workshop, who could call himself a master, how apprentices were trained, and what standards work had to meet.

“So they control their own trade?”

“In part.”

“And if I decide tomorrow that I want to make shoes?”

“You would first spend several years as an apprentice.”

Alaric looked offended.

“I know how leather works.”

“Then you will be an unusually promising apprentice.”

The system amused him until he understood how much of Bellacourt depended on it.

Guilds maintained workshops, trained craftsmen, supported widows of members, contributed money to city defenses and sometimes funded chapels, fountains or hospitals. Their banners appeared in civic processions beside those of nobles and clergy.

Yet they also protected their privileges fiercely.

A young cooper told Alaric that his father had worked barrels for twenty years but would probably never become a master because there were already enough masters in his guild.

“Enough according to whom?” Alaric asked.

The cooper shrugged.

“The masters.”

Bretonnia seemed full of arrangements like that.

Everywhere Alaric found useful institutions wrapped around old advantages.

Knights were expected to defend roads and villages, but they also paid fewer tolls. Noble families were expected to provide soldiers and govern estates, but inherited privileges that ordinary people could never earn. Guilds taught valuable skills, but could close doors behind themselves. The Church ran schools, hospitals, hostels and poor kitchens, yet owned lands and commanded obedience across borders.

Nothing was simple enough to be either good or bad.

At midday they reached the grain market.

Here the scale of the city became obvious.

Sacks were stacked beneath long covered halls. Clerks measured grain. Merchants argued over prices. Wagons waited for inspection before entering warehouses.

Near one gate, a monk and two lay brothers were loading sacks onto a cart marked with the Sacred Lily.

“Where is that going?” Alaric asked.

“To a hospital house in the southern quarter,” said Gaston. “The Church maintains several.”

A little farther on, another Church cart was being filled with dried beans, onions and bread.

Alaric watched it go.

He had spent days hearing Archbishop Anselm speak cautiously about dragons, doctrine and things the Church could not yet understand.

It was easy, from Côte de Lys, to imagine the Church as a collection of old men debating old books.

It looked different here.

A bell sounded from a nearby chapel.

A woman carrying two baskets stopped, crossed herself and continued walking.

A merchant did the same.

So did a soldier.

None of them seemed to think much about it.

The Church was simply there, woven through the city in a thousand small ways.

That afternoon Alaric met King Charles near the merchants’ hall.

The king was inspecting preparations for a public feast connected with the tournament. He moved with only a small escort, though everyone recognized him.

“You have escaped the lists,” Charles said.

“For one day.”

“And what do you think of my capital?”

“It is complicated.”

Charles smiled. “Then you are beginning to understand it.”

They walked together for a time.

Alaric watched a group of pages carrying baskets for an elderly canon. Behind them, two richly dressed merchants argued with a tax clerk. Across the square, a nobleman’s servants cleared a path for his horse.

“In the Valley,” Alaric said, “we argue about who should do something. Here you first argue about who has the right to do it.”

Charles laughed.

“That may be the most accurate criticism of Bretonnia I have heard this year.”

“But people seem to know where they belong.”

“That is the strength of it.”

“And the weakness.”

“Yes.”

Charles did not sound offended.

That surprised Alaric.

They stopped near a fountain where children were filling small buckets.

Beyond them rose the towers of Notre-Dame du Lys.

“The old institutions make us slow,” Charles said. “They also mean that when a road fails, someone knows who must repair it. When a harvest fails, someone knows which granary is responsible. When a lord dies, someone knows who owes protection to the village beneath his walls. The problem begins when people remember the privilege and forget the duty.”

Alaric thought of the vigil in the cathedral.

Strength was supposed to mean responsibility.

Perhaps Bretonnia had built an entire civilization around that idea.

Perhaps it had also spent centuries finding ways to escape it.

Before he could answer, bells began ringing across the city.

Not the cheerful bells that had marked tournament victories.

These were slower.

More deliberate.

Gaston turned toward Notre-Dame du Lys.

Charles’s expression changed.

“What is it?” Alaric asked.

A royal messenger pushed through the crowd toward them.

He bowed quickly.

“Your Majesty. Archbishop Anselm requests your presence at the cathedral. Riders have arrived from the western parishes.”

“Why?”

The messenger hesitated.

“There is sickness.”

The market seemed to continue around them, but Alaric suddenly noticed how many people had stopped to listen to the bells.

Charles looked toward the cathedral.

“Then the tournament can wait.”

## Chapter 6: The Chalice of Saint Armand

By evening, the great hall beside Notre-Dame du Lys no longer looked like a place for ceremonies.

Maps covered the tables.

Letters lay open beneath stones and candlesticks. Priests came and went carrying lists of villages, wells and supplies. Lay brothers counted sacks of grain. Two physicians argued quietly over boiled water. Messengers waited beside the doors with mud still on their boots.

Archbishop Anselm stood at the center of it all.

Alaric had never seen him move so quickly.

“The first reports came five days ago,” Anselm said. “Three villages along the western road. Then seven. Now twelve.”

“What kind of sickness?” Charles asked.

“Fever. Vomiting. Weakness. Mostly among households drawing water from communal wells.”

Alaric looked at the map.

“Then close the wells.”

“We have.”

Anselm tapped several marks with one finger.

“Monasteries are distributing stored water. Carts are bringing barrels from unaffected springs. Priests and brothers are caring for the sick. The king has opened two royal granaries because families who cannot work cannot buy food.”

Another messenger approached and whispered into the Archbishop’s ear.

Anselm nodded.

“Good. Tell both households that the truce stands until the relief roads are clear.”

Alaric glanced at Charles.

“Truce?”

“Two local lords have been quarrelling over a boundary for years,” Charles said quietly. “Apparently neither wishes to explain to the Archbishop why his soldiers prevented water carts from reaching sick children.”

Anselm heard him.

“Nor,” he said, “do they wish to explain it to their own priests.”

Then he returned to the map as if the matter were finished.

Alaric said nothing, but he remembered it.

The Church that could not decide what Vaelor was had, in less than a week, forced two feuding lordships to open roads to one another.

Anselm pointed to an old mark farther west.

“There may be another answer.”

He told them about Saint Armand.

Long before Charles’s kingdom had taken its present shape, Armand had been a knight who abandoned court life to protect pilgrims, travelers and poor villages along dangerous roads. He founded shelters beside wells and springs and taught that a knight who guarded only castles had misunderstood his duty.

After his death, the Church preserved one of the vessels associated with his ministry: a silver chalice used in blessings of communal water.

“The Chalice of Saint Armand,” Anselm said.

Charles frowned.

“I thought it was lost.”

“It was.”

The chalice had once been kept in a remote abbey in the western hills. Generations earlier, a landslide destroyed the road and damaged much of the monastery. The surviving brothers abandoned it. Later attempts to recover the relic failed when the lower halls collapsed further and wild creatures moved into the ruins.

“Convenient,” Alaric said.

Anselm looked at him.

“You doubt the relic?”

“I doubt anything that waits in a ruined abbey full of monsters until exactly the moment someone needs it.”

Charles covered a smile.

Anselm did not.

“The Church has used the Chalice before in times when wells became corrupted. Whether you call its power divine, magical or something your scholars have not yet named is less important to me than the fact that it has worked.”

That answer Alaric understood.

“What guards the abbey?”

“No one knows exactly.”

“Better.”

This time Anselm almost smiled.

The Dragon Riders volunteered before Charles could formally ask.

The following morning, Alaric received a small metal badge bearing the Sacred Lily and the seal of Anselm’s office.

“This identifies you as traveling on Church business connected with the relief effort,” said the canon who gave it to him. “Show it at tolls, bridges, monasteries and lordship boundaries. It should prevent unnecessary delays.”

“Should?”

The canon sighed.

“This is Bretonnia.”

For two days the riders traveled west on horseback.

The sickness became visible before they reached the affected villages.

Church carts passed them carrying water barrels. Monks rode with wagons of bread and dried food. At roadside chapels, lists of sick households had been nailed beside the doors. Local people filled buckets from temporary cisterns while priests marked which families had received their share.

The work was not dramatic.

No swords flashed.

No banners flew.

But it was organized.

At one narrow bridge, armed guards stopped a group of villagers trying to cross from the neighboring lordship. Their own road had become impassable, and the shortest route to an unaffected spring crossed the bridge.

The captain shook his head.

“My orders cover Church carts and named messengers.”

Alaric showed Anselm’s badge.

“They are with me.”

The captain studied the villagers.

“They are not named on your authority.”

“No.”

“Then the seal does not cover them.”

Alaric looked at the empty barrels on their cart.

“They are going to bring water back to sick families.”

The captain hesitated.

“You are asking me to ignore my orders.”

“I am asking you to understand why the orders exist.”

For several seconds neither man moved.

Then the captain stepped aside.

“Go.”

The villagers hurried across.

Alaric nodded once to the guard and continued riding.

No one spoke of it again.

By late afternoon the ruined abbey appeared above the trees.

Its bell tower had lost half its roof. Ivy covered the outer walls. The old road ended beneath a slope of fallen stone, and the riders had to leave their horses below.

Inside, the air smelled of damp earth.

The upper chapel had collapsed years ago, but part of the cloister remained. Water dripped through cracks in the ceiling. Broken statues watched from alcoves filled with moss.

A stairway led downward.

Someone had scratched an old warning into the stone beside it.

Alaric held up his lantern.

“Well,” he said. “At least this part feels familiar.”

The lower halls had become something between monastery and cave.

Roots pushed through walls. Sections of corridor had fallen away into black gaps. Pale spiders the size of dogs nested between broken pillars, and deeper inside the riders found something worse: heavy, hunched creatures with grey skin that had made dens among the old storerooms.

The fighting was short but difficult in the narrow passages.

Without dragons there was no fire from above, no easy retreat into the sky. The riders fought with shields close together, advancing through ruined doorways and across broken floors.

At one point Alaric’s boot went through rotten timber and he nearly vanished into the darkness below.

A companion caught his arm.

“Still prefer horses?” the knight asked.

“No.”

They found the old reliquary beneath what had once been the abbey’s well chapel.

The room was small.

That surprised Alaric.

After the size of Notre-Dame du Lys and the ceremony of Bellacourt, he had expected gold, marble and statues.

Instead there was a stone basin, a faded image of Saint Armand giving water to travelers, and a sealed silver chest built into the wall.

Inside lay the chalice.

It was beautiful, but not grand.

Silver, darkened by age. A narrow band of lilies around the rim. On one side, a tiny engraved knight knelt beside a well while villagers filled their cups.

Alaric lifted it carefully.

For the first time since leaving Bellacourt, he understood why Anselm had spoken of it with such certainty.

The relic did not celebrate a battle.

It celebrated water.

When they returned to the western villages, the Church had already prepared everything.

The sick had been separated from crowded houses. Water from suspect wells was no longer being used. Food and clean barrels had been distributed. Priests, monks, healers and villagers waited together beside the largest communal cistern.

Anselm himself had arrived ahead of them.

He took the Chalice from Alaric without triumph.

“Thank you.”

That was all.

The rite was simple.

Prayers were spoken. Water was drawn into the Chalice and poured back into the cistern. The blessing was repeated at the affected wells over the following days.

Whether the power came from saint, spell or something older, the change was unmistakable.

Cloudiness vanished from the water.

The foul smell disappeared.

New cases of sickness began to fall.

Within days, families were drawing water from their wells again.

Alaric watched a little girl fill a cup and drink.

Her mother began to cry.

There were no trumpets.

No tournament crowd.

No one shouted the Archbishop’s name.

The monks simply moved on to the next village.

That evening Alaric found Anselm beside one of the restored wells.

“You were right about one thing,” Alaric said.

The Archbishop looked suspicious.

“Only one?”

“Your Church is more than old books.”

Anselm looked toward the villagers carrying water home.

“And your dragons, perhaps, are more than old fears.”

Alaric turned toward him.

But Anselm had already started walking back toward the chapel.

It was not acceptance.

Not yet.

Still, it was the first crack in the wall between them.

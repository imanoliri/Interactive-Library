# Valley of Dragons IV: Land of Chivalry

> Fresh prose draft. Chapters 1–2 only. This draft is production material and is not accepted canon until reviewed.

## Chapter 1: The Invitation

Spring had returned to the Valley of Dragons.

Snow still shone on the highest peaks, but below them the roads were open again. Carts rolled between the human towns, smoke rose from the new dwarven settlement beneath the western hills, and the forests had begun to turn green. Above it all, dragons crossed the sky in long, unhurried arcs.

From the walls of Castle Tarn, Alaric watched Vaelor descend toward the courtyard.

The sapphire-blue dragon landed with a deep beat of his wings. Dust and loose straw swept across the stones. A moment later one of the gate guards came running from below.

“Sir Alaric! A visitor has arrived.”

Alaric smiled. “We have visitors every day.”

“Not like this one.”

That proved true.

The stranger waiting in the great hall wore no armor for war, yet almost everything about him announced rank. His riding cloak was dark blue and carefully cut. A silver chain crossed his chest. At his side hung a sword whose polished hilt had probably spent more time in ceremonies than in battle. Most striking of all was the embroidered lily upon his coat.

He bowed when Alaric entered.

“Sir Alaric of the Dragon Rider Order?”

“I am Alaric.”

The man straightened. “Gaston, royal herald in the service of His Majesty Charles the Third, King of Bretonnia.”

Alaric glanced toward the other knights gathered in the hall. The name meant nothing to him.

Gaston seemed to notice.

“That,” he said with a small smile, “is one reason I have ridden so far.”

He produced a long parchment sealed with blue wax and the mark of a crown and lily.

Alaric broke the seal.

The letter was courteous, elaborate, and unlike the practical messages that usually reached Castle Tarn. King Charles III had heard of the Dragon Rider Order: of the war against the Dark God, of the strange winter that had threatened the Valley, and of the riders’ recent campaign beneath the mountains. He invited representatives of the Order to his court in Bellacourt, capital of the Kingdom of Bretonnia, and to take part in a Grand Tourney attended by knights and nobles from across the Bretonnian Lands.

Alaric read the final lines twice.

“Where exactly is Bretonnia?” he asked.

Gaston looked surprised, then amused rather than offended.

“Far west of here. Beyond lands your people trade with, I expect. Bretonnia is both a kingdom and a name older than the kingdom. Our king rules one of the greatest realms of the Bretonnian Lands, but many other kings, dukes, bishops, counts, cities and lords rule lands of their own.”

“So Charles is not king of all of them?”

“No.” Gaston paused. “Though His Majesty would say they are all brothers.”

That answer interested Alaric more than the tournament.

By evening, the invitation had become the subject of a council at Castle Tarn.

Some knights saw little reason to travel so far merely to knock other knights from horses. Others disagreed. The Valley had spent most of its history looking inward. That had begun to change. Dwarven ambassadors now walked its roads. Traders brought stories from beyond the mountains. Even Gaston’s presence was proof that the wider world had begun to notice them.

“If a king sends a herald across half the known world,” Alaric said, “the tournament is not the only reason.”

One of the older riders nodded. “He wants to see us.”

“And to be seen receiving us,” another said.

Gaston, who had been invited to speak openly, did not deny it.

“The Grand Tourney is one of the great gatherings of our lands. Lords who quarrel over borders still meet beneath the same banners. Knights travel for weeks to compete. Clergy, merchants and ambassadors attend. His Majesty believes the Dragon Rider Order belongs among them.”

“Why?” Alaric asked.

The herald considered his answer.

“Because Charles believes the Bretonnian Lands have spent too many generations divided by old rivalries. He believes strong realms should know one another instead of fearing one another. And because your Order has become famous without asking anyone’s permission to become so.”

There was enough diplomacy in the answer to make several knights laugh.

Later, after the council had ended, Alaric climbed to the upper wall. Vaelor rested on the grass beyond the castle, his blue scales pale beneath the moon.

Alaric had crossed mountains, frozen forests and caverns beneath the earth. Yet the thought of riding into a land of courts, tournaments, bishops and dozens of competing rulers felt strangely unfamiliar.

Enemies were often simple. They attacked, and you defended.

A royal invitation was more difficult.

He looked west.

“We should go,” he said.

Vaelor raised his head.

Within days, the answer bearing Castle Tarn’s seal was placed in Gaston’s hands.

The Dragon Rider Order would accept the invitation of King Charles III.

For the first time, Alaric would travel beyond the lands he knew not to fight a monster or answer a call for help, but simply to meet another civilization.

He would soon discover that in Bretonnia, a tournament could be as political as a battle.

## Chapter 2: Bellacourt

The first thing Alaric learned about the Bretonnian Lands was that Gaston had not exaggerated their divisions.

During the long journey west, borders appeared where Alaric could barely see anything worth dividing. A stone bridge belonged to one lord, the fields beyond it to another. At one town, Gaston exchanged formal greetings with the guards of a duke. Two days later they passed through land governed by an abbey. Elsewhere they followed a road maintained by a free city whose banners flew from every tollhouse.

Yet the differences sat beside something equally obvious: the people belonged to the same world.

The forms of their churches resembled one another. Knights carried different coats of arms but followed the same elaborate courtesies. Market cries changed in accent more than language. Travelers crossed borders to attend the same feast days, monasteries answered to authorities beyond the lands of local rulers, and stories of famous knights seemed to belong to everyone.

“It is all one country,” Alaric said one evening, “except that it isn’t.”

Gaston laughed.

“That is a very foreign way of understanding Bretonnia, Sir Alaric.”

“Is it wrong?”

“Not entirely.”

The Kingdom of Bretonnia announced itself more gradually than Alaric expected. There was no wall across the countryside. Instead the roads improved. Royal lilies appeared more often beside the arms of local lords. Patrols became more frequent. Villages grew larger, then gave way to prosperous towns connected by busy roads.

And finally they saw Bellacourt.

The capital spread before them in walls, towers, roofs and crowded streets, larger than any human city Alaric had seen in the Valley. Bells sounded somewhere within. Banners hung from towers in preparation for the Grand Tourney. Along the roads outside the gates stood camps belonging to visiting nobles whose servants, horses, wagons and guards made small towns of their own.

Vaelor and the other dragons caused all of it to stop.

People abandoned carts in the road. Children pointed upward. Horses fought their reins. Guards on the walls shouted to one another as the dragons descended toward the fields outside the city.

Alaric had expected surprise. He had not expected fear.

Some citizens stared with wonder. Others crossed themselves in the Bretonnian manner Gaston had shown them. A priest near the gate pulled two children behind him.

Gaston’s expression tightened.

“They have never seen dragons,” Alaric said.

“Most have never believed they would.”

“Your king invited Dragon Riders.”

“Yes.” Gaston looked toward the cathedral towers rising above the city. “The king did.”

That was the first warning.

Inside Bellacourt, Alaric found the city almost overwhelming. The Valley’s towns mixed farmers, craftsmen and merchants without much concern for ceremony. Bellacourt seemed to give every part of society its own place, clothing and rules.

Guild signs hung above workshops. Merchants occupied whole streets. Noble households moved through the crowds beneath their colors. Priests and monks were everywhere. Pages hurried behind knights carrying helmets and shields. Squires led decorated horses toward the tournament grounds. Foreign delegations filled inns and courtyards, their heraldry announcing realms Alaric had never heard of.

The city itself appeared to be preparing for a festival and a diplomatic congress at the same time.

At its center stood Côte de Lys, the royal palace and citadel of Charles III. Not far away rose the great cathedral commonly called Notre-Dame du Lys—the Cathedral of Our Lady of the Sacred Lily—whose bells could be heard across Bellacourt.

Alaric noticed that people spoke of the two places differently.

The palace belonged to the king.

The cathedral, somehow, belonged to something larger.

Their formal audience took place at Côte de Lys.

King Charles III was not what Alaric had expected from Gaston’s careful speeches. He was richly dressed, certainly, and surrounded by enough ceremony to make even an experienced knight uncertain where to stand. But when the formal greetings ended, Charles came down from the raised place where he had received them and spoke to Alaric almost eagerly.

“I have wanted to see one of your dragons since I first heard the stories,” he said.

“Vaelor would probably say the same about your tournament horses.”

Charles laughed.

For a time the conversation was easy. Charles asked about Castle Tarn, the dwarves beneath the western mountains, the organization of the Dragon Rider Order and the peoples of the Valley. Alaric asked about the extraordinary patchwork of realms he had crossed.

Charles did not hide his opinion of it.

“We share a faith, a history, a language and the same ideas of knighthood,” he said. “Yet a merchant may pass through six jurisdictions in a week, and six rulers may quarrel while an enemy gathers beyond them. We have spent centuries proving how different brothers can be.”

“You want to rule them?” Alaric asked.

Several courtiers went very still.

Charles smiled.

“I want them to remember that they are brothers.”

It was not an answer, and it was.

Before Alaric could press him, another delegation entered the chamber.

At its head walked Archbishop Anselm.

The churchman was elderly but not frail. His robes were magnificent, his manner controlled, and the nobles around him made room without being asked. Alaric understood immediately that the Archbishop possessed a kind of authority that did not come from soldiers.

Anselm welcomed the visitors from the Valley politely.

Then he asked about the dragons.

“They remain outside the inner city,” Charles said.

“For now,” said Anselm.

Alaric looked from the Archbishop to the king.

“Is there a problem?”

Anselm answered directly.

“There is uncertainty.”

“They are dragons.”

“Precisely.”

Alaric almost smiled, but Anselm was serious.

The Archbishop explained that Bretonnian scripture and tradition contained accounts of many strange beings: some natural, some monstrous, some symbols of temptation or destruction. Dragons such as Vaelor were unknown to Bretonnian scholars. The Church had received reports of enormous winged creatures capable of speech, magic and fire, bonded to armed knights.

“And you decided they were evil?” Alaric asked.

“I said we do not know what they are.”

“I know what Vaelor is.”

“You have known him for years. We have known of him for weeks.”

That answer irritated Alaric precisely because it was not completely unreasonable.

Anselm continued.

“The Church has obligations beyond this court. What is accepted in Bellacourt will be discussed in monasteries, bishoprics and kingdoms hundreds of leagues apart. I cannot declare a creature harmless because a famous knight tells me it is so.”

“And I cannot insult my dragon because your books do not mention him.”

Charles stepped between them before the disagreement hardened.

“No one is asking you to condemn your companions.”

Anselm looked displeased, but allowed the king to continue.

Charles explained the difficulty. Clergy were already protesting the dragons’ presence. Visiting nobles had brought priests and bishops of their own. Rumors were spreading through Bellacourt faster than any official proclamation could answer them. If Charles simply overruled the Church, the Grand Tourney could become a religious dispute rather than the diplomatic gathering he intended.

“What are you asking?” Alaric said.

Charles hesitated.

“That the dragons return to the Valley while you and your knights remain as my guests.”

Alaric stared at him.

“We crossed half the world together.”

“I know.”

“And now you want us to send them away because your priests are nervous.”

Anselm’s face hardened.

“Because an institution responsible for the spiritual life of millions of people does not change doctrine in an afternoon.”

There it was: the strength of the Church and its weakness in the same sentence.

It connected kingdoms that could agree on little else. It kept records, educated scholars, maintained monasteries and gave distant peoples a shared language of belief. But because it carried centuries behind it, it moved with the weight of centuries.

Alaric asked for time.

That evening he left Bellacourt and went to Vaelor.

The dragon listened without interrupting as Alaric explained the king’s request.

When he finished, Vaelor gave a low rumble.

“They fear what they do not understand,” Alaric said.

Vaelor lowered his great head until one blue eye was level with him.

“Yes,” Alaric said. “I know. Humans do that often.”

The decision angered him, but the Order had not come to Bretonnia to begin a conflict with its Church. If they remained, perhaps they could show these people what kind of knights rode dragons—even without the dragons beside them.

The following morning Alaric gave Charles his answer.

The dragons would return to the Valley.

The riders would stay.

From a tower of Côte de Lys, the court watched the dragons depart eastward, their wings growing smaller against the morning sky until they disappeared.

Archbishop Anselm watched as well.

Alaric wondered what he saw when he looked at them: monsters, mysteries, or simply a question his Church had not yet learned how to answer.

Sooner or later, Alaric thought, Bretonnia would have to decide.

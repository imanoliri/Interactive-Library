# Valley of Dragons IV: Land of Chivalry

## Chapter 11: Honor and Reward

Bellacourt did not celebrate immediately.

For two days after Lothar’s army withdrew, the city repaired walls, buried the dead, treated the wounded and counted what had been lost.

The tournament grounds became a camp for soldiers and refugees. Guild halls that had made arrows and shields now made doors, beams and carts. Priests moved between hospitals and funeral rites. Masons inspected every cracked tower along the eastern wall.

The dragons remained outside the city at first.

Not because anyone ordered them away.

There was simply nowhere sensible to put them.

That problem amused Alaric more than it should have.

On the third morning, Vaelor landed in a broad meadow beyond the western wall while children, soldiers and half the city seemed to gather at a cautious distance.

Some stared.

Some crossed themselves.

Some cheered.

A few did all three.

Archbishop Anselm came as well.

He stopped several paces from Vaelor.

The great sapphire-blue dragon lowered his head.

For a moment neither moved.

Then Anselm bowed.

Not deeply.

But unmistakably.

“Welcome to Bellacourt,” he said.

Vaelor looked at Alaric.

“He is learning,” Alaric said.

Anselm heard him.

“So are you.”

That afternoon, the bells of Notre-Dame du Lys rang for thanksgiving.

The cathedral doors stood open.

Knights entered beside guildmasters. Farmers stood near merchants. Soldiers filled the aisles alongside monks and clerks. The king sat beneath no private canopy.

The dragons could not enter, of course.

Vaelor waited in the square outside with several others, their shadows passing over the cathedral windows whenever they shifted their wings.

Anselm spoke before the congregation.

He did not pretend that every question had been answered.

He did something harder.

He admitted that the Church had judged before it understood.

“We were right,” he said, “to ask whether great power can serve good or evil. We were wrong to believe that unfamiliarity itself was evidence of corruption.”

The cathedral was silent.

“The dragons of the Valley defended this city when they had every reason to remember our suspicion rather than our need. We will remember what they chose.”

Alaric looked through the open doors toward Vaelor.

The dragon was trying to steal an apple from a merchant’s cart without moving his feet.

Alaric decided not to mention it.

After the service, King Charles summoned Alaric, Raymond, Anselm and the Dragon Rider delegation to Côte de Lys.

The great hall had been repaired enough for ceremony, though one damaged window was still covered with boards.

A long map lay across the central table.

Alaric recognized the kingdom.

Charles pointed to a place several days from Bellacourt, where forest met open farmland and a river bent southward between low hills.

“There is an estate here,” the king said.

Alaric looked at him.

“That sounds dangerous.”

Raymond laughed.

Charles ignored both of them.

“It is not a duchy.”

“Good.”

“It is not even a large barony.”

“Better.”

“It is a manor and its dependent lands.”

Charles tapped the map.

“There is a fortified house of stone, old but sound, with a square defensive tower and a walled court. The manor includes fields, meadow, woodland and several tenant holdings. A river forms part of its boundary.”

Gaston unrolled a second parchment.

“There are fishing rights on the river, timber rights in part of the forest, and hunting rights in the royal wood adjoining the estate.”

Raymond leaned over the map.

“There are deer.”

Charles looked at him.

“That is your contribution?”

“I considered it important.”

“There are also boar,” Gaston said.

Raymond nodded approvingly.

Alaric examined the parchment.

The holding was not enormous.

That was what made it interesting.

A manor house. A tower. A few villages and scattered farmsteads owing rents, labor or service according to local custom. Fields sufficient to feed the household. Meadows for horses. A forest for timber and hunting. A river for water, fish and transport.

It was small enough to understand.

Large enough to matter.

“What would we owe you?” Alaric asked.

Charles smiled.

That was clearly the question he had been waiting for.

“Protection of the estate and its people. Maintenance of the house and tower. Respect for the rights already held by the villagers and tenants. Hospitality to royal messengers when reasonably required.”

“And soldiers?”

“A small feudal obligation in theory.”

“In theory?”

“In practice, if the Dragon Rider Order answers a call to defend the kingdom, I doubt anyone will complain that you failed to send six spearmen and a cart.”

Alaric nodded.

“And the people living there become our subjects?”

“Not property,” Charles said immediately.

“Good.”

“They owe customary rents and duties to the lordship, and the lordship owes them protection, justice and the maintenance of certain common rights. Some households hold land directly. Some owe labor at harvest. Others pay in coin or produce. There are free tenants as well as dependent ones.”

Alaric looked again at the map.

“So this is not just a house.”

“No,” said Charles.

“It is responsibility.”

Anselm, standing nearby, smiled faintly.

Alaric noticed.

“You planned that.”

Charles looked innocent.

“I have no idea what you mean.”

The king explained that the estate had belonged to a noble family whose line had ended years earlier. The Crown had administered it since then through stewards. Its fortified house guarded a useful river crossing and an old road between the interior and the western approaches.

“It would become a chapter house of the Dragon Rider Order,” Charles said. “A permanent place in Bretonnia. Your knights may stay there, train there, receive messengers there and travel from there when needed.”

Alaric imagined it.

A Dragon Rider banner above a square stone tower.

Horses in the meadow.

Vaelor sleeping beside a river.

Local children learning that dragons were not creatures from sermons or stories but neighbors who occasionally stole livestock if insufficiently supervised.

“It will need a larger courtyard,” he said.

“For the horses?” Raymond asked.

“For the dragons.”

Charles stared at the map.

“Yes. That may require some architectural imagination.”

“And fireproof roofs,” Gaston added.

“Probably.”

Anselm cleared his throat.

“I can recommend masons.”

Alaric looked at him.

“Church masons?”

“They build in stone.”

“Because of dragons?”

“Because of fires.”

“Caused by dragons?”

Anselm sighed.

“Occasionally.”

The ceremony took place the next morning in the inner court of Côte de Lys.

Charles stood beneath the royal standard. Raymond stood at his right, alive, free and visibly pleased with himself. Anselm stood to the king’s left.

The Dragon Riders gathered opposite them.

A clerk read the grant aloud.

It took much longer than Alaric expected.

The document listed fields, woods, riverbanks, mills, paths, rents, rights, duties, boundaries, hunting privileges, fishing privileges, rights to fallen timber, rights of passage and several arguments involving a meadow that apparently had troubled local officials for sixty years.

Alaric leaned toward Gaston.

“Does anyone understand all of this?”

“The steward will.”

“We get a steward?”

“You will need one.”

“That sounds expensive.”

“You own a manor now.”

Alaric was beginning to understand why nobles always looked tired.

At last the clerk finished.

Charles placed his hand on the sealed charter.

“This estate is granted to the Dragon Rider Order in recognition of service to the Kingdom of Bretonnia and its people, to be held under the obligations named here and to serve as a permanent house of the Order within our realm.”

He looked at Alaric.

“Do you accept it?”

Alaric thought of the market.

The guilds.

The pages and squires.

The Church hospitals.

The western wells.

The guarded bridge.

The ruined abbey.

Raymond’s empty stable.

The siege walls.

The people who had carried arrows, water, stones and wounded strangers because someone had to.

When he first arrived in Bretonnia, privilege had been the easiest thing to see.

Now he understood the other half of the bargain.

Duty.

Not always fulfilled.

Not always fair.

But real.

“We accept,” he said.

Charles handed him the charter.

Then Anselm stepped forward.

He held no relic and performed no elaborate rite.

He simply placed one hand over the sealed document.

“May this house defend those who live beneath its protection,” he said. “May its strength never become an excuse for arrogance. May those who hold authority there remember why authority is given.”

Alaric looked at him.

“That one I like.”

“I thought you might.”

Outside the court, Vaelor roared.

Every horse in Côte de Lys objected at once.

The ceremony ended somewhat less gracefully than intended.

Several weeks later, before returning fully to the Valley, Alaric visited the estate.

The fortified house stood on a low rise above the river.

It was smaller than Castle Tarn and much less impressive than Côte de Lys.

Alaric liked it immediately.

A square tower rose from one corner of the stone house. A curtain wall enclosed a yard, stable, granary and workshops. Beyond it lay orchards and fields.

The river curved below the hill, broad enough for small boats and shallow enough in places to ford.

On the opposite bank began the forest.

A steward met them at the gate with several local tenants and two nervous village elders.

They had clearly heard stories about dragons.

Vaelor landed in the meadow instead of the courtyard.

The elders relaxed slightly.

Then Vaelor spotted a herd of deer at the forest edge.

Alaric pointed at him.

“No.”

Vaelor looked offended.

Raymond, who had accompanied them, whispered, “Technically, you have hunting rights.”

“That does not mean he eats the entire forest.”

“I was merely observing the legal position.”

The steward began explaining the manor.

There were tenant farms along the river.

A small mill downstream.

Common pasture shared by several households.

Woodland where villagers collected deadfall by custom.

Fishing pools leased to local families.

A hunting lodge in poor repair.

The tower roof leaked.

One barn needed rebuilding.

Two boundary stones had disappeared.

A bridge toll had not been collected correctly for three years.

Alaric listened.

The list grew.

Finally he laughed.

“What?” Raymond asked.

“When Charles gave us this place, I thought the tower was the important part.”

“And now?”

Alaric looked down toward the river.

Children were watching Vaelor from behind a fence.

A farmer repaired a gate beside his son.

Smoke rose from cottage chimneys among the trees.

A woman carried baskets toward the mill.

The fortified house was only the center.

The manor was everything around it.

“The people are the important part.”

Raymond nodded.

“That is usually the part nobles learn last.”

Alaric looked at him.

“You are a noble.”

“Yes.”

“Did you learn it last?”

Raymond considered.

“I am still learning.”

They walked to the tower.

From its roof Alaric could see the entire holding: river, fields, forest, road, scattered farms and the distant line of hills.

It was not a kingdom.

It was not even a great lordship.

But it was a place where the Valley and Bretonnia now touched permanently.

A messenger could arrive there asking for help.

A wounded knight could recover there.

A dragon could sleep in the meadow.

A child from the manor could grow up seeing that the creatures once feared by the Church could also guard the road home.

Vaelor landed beside the river below and folded his wings.

For the first time since arriving in the Bretonnian Lands, Alaric did not feel like a visitor.

He felt responsible for a piece of it.
